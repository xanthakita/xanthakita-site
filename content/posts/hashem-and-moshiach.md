*This grew out of a long back-and-forth with Claude while I was reading Colossians 3 in the Orthodox Jewish Bible. I asked the questions; the working-out below is largely Claude's, checked against the text. I'm keeping it here because I want to be able to find it again.*

## 1. Two names, kept apart

In this translation you meet both **Hashem** and **Moshiach**, and they are not the same being. The translation is built to keep that visible.

**Hashem** (הַשֵּׁם) means "the Name." It is the standard Jewish reverential substitute for the four-letter divine name, YHWH, which observant Jews do not pronounce. Wherever a conventional English Bible prints *God* or *the LORD* in reference to the Father, this translation prints Hashem. The same reverence produces its *G-d* and *L-rd* spellings.

**Moshiach** (מָשִׁיחַ) means "anointed one." It is the Hebrew word English renders *Messiah* and Greek renders *Christos*, *Christ*. Every place a familiar translation says "Christ," this one says "Moshiach." The fuller title in this translation is *Rebbe, Melech HaMoshiach* — Rabbi, King Messiah — identified as Yehoshua.

You can watch the distinction operate across Colossians 3:

- **v. 1** — Moshiach is seated *at the right hand of* Hashem. Two parties, one seated beside the other.
- **v. 3** — your life is hidden *with* Moshiach *in* Hashem.
- **v. 6** — the wrath is Hashem's.
- **v. 12** — believers are the chosen *of* Hashem.
- **v. 17** — thanks are given *to* Hashem, *through* the Moshiach.

The consistent grammar is **through or with Moshiach → to Hashem**. Moshiach is the mediating figure; Hashem is the one mediated to.

One note on the translation itself. The OJB's vocabulary is Jewish; its theology is Christian. It equates Moshiach with Yehoshua and gives him a status shared with God. Traditional Judaism uses *Moshiach* very differently: a future human king descended from David who restores Israel and the Temple — fully mortal, not divine, and not Jesus. The term is shared across the two; the referent is not.

## 2. Judgment handed over

Here is the apparent problem. Colossians 3:6 makes the wrath Hashem's, while John 5:22 says the Father judges *no one*.

Read closely, John 5 is the chapter that most explicitly *builds* the pattern rather than breaking it. It simply states it in legal language instead of liturgical language. Everything the Son has in that chapter, he has because it was given:

- **v. 19** — the Son can do nothing of himself; he does what he *sees* the Father doing
- **v. 20** — the Father *shows* him all that he does
- **v. 22** — judgment has been *given* to him, the whole of it
- **v. 26** — the Father has life in himself; he *granted* the Son to have life in himself
- **v. 27** — he *gave* him authority to execute judgment, "because he is the Son of Man"
- **v. 30** — "I can do nothing on my own… I seek not my own will but the will of him who *sent* me"

Not one of these claims independent possession. The chapter opens with the accusation of v. 18 — that he was making himself equal with God — and his entire answer is a denial of autonomy. So v. 22 does not mean the Father has stopped judging. It means he no longer judges *directly*: he judges through an appointed agent, and v. 30 says the verdict still originates upstream — "as I hear, I judge."

The legal category here is the **shaliach** (שָׁלִיחַ), the agent. *Shlucho shel adam k'moto* — a man's agent is like the man himself (Kiddushin 41b). An agent acting within his commission carries the sender's full authority, and slighting the agent slights the sender. That is precisely the logic of v. 23: honor the Son as you honor the Father, because dishonoring the sent one dishonors the one who sent him. A *melech* appoints a judge; the judge's verdict *is* the king's verdict, without the judge being the king.

Two texts stand behind the scene. **Daniel 7:13–14** is cued by "because he is the Son of Man" in v. 27 — one like a son of man approaches the Ancient of Days and *is given* dominion, glory, and a kingdom. That is itself a two-figure scene: one enthroned figure hands authority to another. And **Psalm 110:1** stands behind "the right hand of Hashem" in Colossians 3:1 — the enthronement of a viceroy. The right hand means delegated royal authority, and it means a second seat.

So the two chapters are drawing one diagram in two vocabularies:

| | Colossians 3 | John 5 |
|---|---|---|
| **Source** | Hashem — the wrath is his (v. 6); thanks go to him (v. 17) | The Father — sends, gives, shows |
| **Administrator** | Moshiach — at his right hand (v. 1); things done *through* him (v. 17) | The Son — given all judgment (v. 22) |

The rest of the New Testament reads it the same way. Acts 17:31 has God judging the world through a man he appointed; Romans 2:16 has God judging the secrets of men through Messiah. Ownership and administration are distinct, and the delegation is what lets both statements stand.

Where the tension is real: John 5 pushes past ordinary agency at two points. An ordinary *shaliach* does not receive the honor due the sender (v. 23), and v. 26 grants the Son the capacity to have life *in himself* — a divine attribute, granted rather than merely borrowed. That is why the accusation of v. 18 is in the text at all. The narrative records that his hearers heard something more than delegation.

## 3. Through, and in

Then the question that turned the whole thread for me: does the Father judge us *by using* the Son, or by looking at us *through the lens* of the Son?

English "through" does at least three different jobs, and the Greek behind these passages picks exactly one of them:

| Sense | Example | Greek |
|---|---|---|
| Instrumental | I sent word *through* my assistant — a channel, an agent | διά + genitive |
| Perceptual | I saw the room *through* the window — a lens | not in play here |
| Causal | Cancelled *through* no fault of mine — on account of | διά + accusative |

The control text is 1 Corinthians 8:6: all things ἐκ the Father — out of, from, source — and all things διά the Son — through, agency. The preposition pair is doing deliberate work: origin against mediation. Same in John 1:3 and Hebrews 1:2. In each case διά marks the one **by whose action** something is done, never the one **through whom something is viewed**. Romans 2:16 puts judgment in that same construction.

And John 5:22 does not use "through" at all. The verb is δίδωμι — the Father has *handed over* the whole of judgment. Verse 27 repeats it. That is a transfer of office, not an optical arrangement. The Son is not the window the Father looks through; he is the one on the bench.

On the narrow question, then, the grammar will not carry the lens: **the Father judges by using the Son.**

But the lens idea is not wrong — it is just filed under a different preposition. The New Testament says something very close to "the Father sees us through the Son," and it says it with ἐν, *in*. Colossians 3 is one of the densest places for it:

- **v. 3** — your life is hidden *with* Moshiach *in* Hashem
- **v. 4** — when Moshiach appears, you appear *with* him in glory
- **v. 10–12** — you have *put on* the new man; *put on*, as the chosen of Hashem, compassion and humility

That last is the giveaway. The verb is ἐνδύω, to get dressed. Galatians 3:27 says it flatly — those immersed into Messiah have *put him on* like a garment; Romans 13:14 the same. Ephesians 1:6: accepted *in the Beloved*. Romans 8:1: no condemnation for those *in* Messiah. Not a medium of sight but a location and a garment. A covering.

The Hebrew courtroom version of this is Zechariah 3. Yehoshua the Kohen Gadol stands before the angel of Hashem; *ha-satan*, the accuser, stands at his right hand to accuse him. He is dressed in filthy garments. The verdict is not reached by examining the garments. The filthy ones are removed, clean ones are put on him, and *then* he stands. Covering, not agency — inside a judgment scene. The Hebrew instinct is not optical either: *kapparah* (כַּפָּרָה), atonement, comes from a root meaning *to cover*. Not "God looks through something at me" but "something is over me when God looks."

So there were two questions hiding inside my one question:

| Question | Answer | Preposition |
|---|---|---|
| Who renders the verdict? | The Son, by delegated authority | διά / "given" — John 5:22, Rom 2:16 |
| What does the Father see? | The one clothed in, hidden in, located in the Son | ἐν / σύν — Col 3:3, Rom 8:1, Gal 3:27 |

They are not rival answers to one question. And they interlock: the same person who occupies the bench is the garment the accused is wearing.

Worth flagging that "looking at us through the lens of the Son" is closer to Reformation phrasing — imputed righteousness, Luther's *alien righteousness*, the wedding garment — than to first-century idiom. It is a real doctrine with real texts, but it is built from the ἐν Χριστῷ passages, not from the word *through*.

## 4. Union as the ground of both

Which is where I landed: what if being in Moshiach does both — changes what the Father sees *and* changes what you are?

That turns out to be the position Calvin actually held, and it is not what most people mean when they say "Protestant." He called it *duplex gratia*, double grace: justification and sanctification, both received in union with Messiah, distinguishable but inseparable — and, crucially, **neither one the cause of the other**. Both flow from the union. That is a stronger claim than "both happen," and it is what makes "both" more than a hedge.

Once you say both, the ordering becomes the whole question:

| Ground | Claim | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Transformation first | God declares righteous because he has made righteous (roughly Trent) | The verdict waits on the process — yet Romans 4:5 has him justifying *the ungodly*, before any process has run |
| Verdict first | Declared righteous, therefore progressively made so (popular Protestantism) | Looks like God calling true what is not yet true — the "legal fiction" charge |
| Union grounds both | Neither derives from the other; both are aspects of one reality (Calvin's *duplex gratia*) | Dissolves the fiction charge — the declaration is true because the union is real, not because the process is finished |

Colossians 3 already refuses the split. Look at what it does in nine verses:

- **v. 3** — you *died*; your life *is hidden* with Moshiach in Hashem. Accomplished. A status.
- **v. 5** — *therefore* put to death what is earthly in you. Ongoing. A process.
- **v. 10** — you *have put on* the new man.
- **v. 12** — *put on* compassion, humility, patience.

Twice the chapter states the accomplished fact and then, on the strength of a *therefore*, commands the process. Paul never argues from one to the other as cause and effect; he treats them as two faces of one thing. Romans 6 does it identically — you died with him, so *reckon* yourselves dead, so *do not let* sin reign. 1 Corinthians 6:11 stacks washed, sanctified, justified in one breath with no ordering at all.

And the garment had already said both. In the Hebrew frame, garments do not only cover — they **confer office**. Exodus 28: the Kohen Gadol's garments are made "for glory and for beauty," and he is consecrated *by being clothed*. He is not a priest who then gets dressed; the vesting is the ordination. Zechariah 3 does both in one motion — the filthy garments come off, the clean ones go on, and then he is given a charge, a court to walk in, a standing among those present. Covering and commissioning in a single act. The scene never pauses to ask which was the real thing.

What "both" still has to answer: almost everyone affirms both. Trent says God genuinely transforms; Westminster says God genuinely declares. The historic argument was never whether he does both. It was — **at the moment of judgment, which one is the verdict resting on?** The question has teeth where transformation is visibly incomplete: someone in Messiah whose life shows little change, or who dies mid-process. The forensic answer says the verdict never rested there. The transformative answer needs a mechanism for completion, which is where purgatory enters the Catholic system. Holding both does not exempt you from answering; it means answering from inside the union rather than from one half.

And this is exactly where John 5 left off. Verse 22 hands the whole verdict to the Son, and seven verses later verse 29 sorts the resurrection by *those who have done good* and those who have done evil. Delegated authority and deeds-language, in one chapter, unreconciled on the surface.

## 5. Where readings part

I want to be honest about what is settled here and what is not.

**On John 5 and the Son's status.** The trinitarian reading takes v. 19 and v. 30 as describing the Son's role within his mission — functional subordination — while v. 18, v. 23 and v. 26 describe his nature: one being, two persons. The unitarian or agency reading takes the "given" language as the plain sense throughout; the honor of v. 23 is agent-honor, and v. 18 records an accusation the text does not endorse. The traditional Jewish reading takes v. 18 as precisely the claim Judaism rejects: Moshiach is a mortal Davidic king who restores Israel, he does not share God's judgment seat, and Deuteronomy 6:4 with Isaiah 43:11 closes the question.

All three agree on one thing. John 5 does not merge the two terms. Whatever else the chapter is doing, Hashem and Moshiach remain two parties throughout — one sending, one sent.

**On being "in Moshiach."** Reformed readings take it as a change in how God regards you, a righteousness reckoned from outside. Catholic and Orthodox readings take it as actual participation and transformation, righteousness infused rather than imputed. The fault line runs directly through the question of what the union changes: what the Father *sees*, or what you *are*.

One last thing, which I did not expect. Some of this argument is downstream of vocabulary. Greek δικαιόω leans declarative — to pronounce righteous. The Latin that rendered it, *iustificare*, literally means *to make righteous*, and that verb shaped a thousand years of Western theology before the Reformers went back behind it to the Greek. Hebrew never forced the choice: *tzedakah* (צְדָקָה) is rightness-in-relationship, covenant faithfulness — inherently both a standing and a way of living.

A translation reaching back past the Latin categories may be recovering something rather than synthesizing it.
